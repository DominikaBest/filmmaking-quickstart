SHOTS & CUTS / 03

The camera is
where you put our attention.

Choose a shot for what the viewer needs to understand—not just because it looks cinematic.

Size, angle and movement are different choices.

SIZE

How much do we see?

Wide for relationships and space. Medium for action and exchange. Close for a specific change in a face.

ANGLE

Where are we looking from?

Eye level meets a person. A low angle can emphasize presence; a high angle can reveal exposure or layout. Context decides the meaning.

MOVEMENT

Why does the camera move?

Follow an action, reveal information or change our distance from a person. Stillness is a valid choice too.

→ TrackMove with a walking character.
↗ Push inMove closer as a decision lands.
↔ PanTurn to reveal who heard it.

CONTINUITY SAVES THE SCENE

Draw the room
before you draw the shots.

Keep screen direction and eyelines consistent. Stay on one side of the action axis until you intentionally re-establish the geography.

Moving the camera must not move the door.

The Empty Chair: two separate desks, two possible seats, one readable choice.

Cut on the choice

He hears the insult → looks at the offered chair → chooses the other seat. Don’t skip the decision that makes the action meaningful.

Keep action continuous

If a hand starts reaching in the wide, pick up the same phase in the closer shot. Track which hand holds which object.

Let sound connect

Carry room tone across cuts. A chair scrape or a stopped pencil can mark a turn. Dialogue must remain intelligible.

FRAME SHAPE

9:16 emphasizes vertical space and faces. 16:9 gives room for relationships side by side. 2.39:1 can emphasize lateral distance. Compose for the format from the start; cropping can erase the important chair.

THE VIRAL-TEASER TOOLKIT / 04

Make a promise.
Then change its meaning.

A flip turns an expectation into an emotionally meaningful choice. The audience should see the reversal happen.

UNIVERSAL FANTASY · The hottest guy in the room chooses you

“She doesn’t belong.”→He chooses the seat beside her.

The Empty Chair: earn the choice before the flip

An example, not a universal formula
THE FLIP IS A CHOICE“He sits down” is an action. “He passes the offered seat to sit beside the excluded girl” changes the social meaning.

Hook: start with pressure

A clear exclusion, contradiction or risky desire gives us a question. Open with the meaningful moment, not a tour of the setting.

Payoff: show proof

Make allegiance visible through action. Then show its effect on the person who receives it.

Aftermath: leave desire

Resolve the small moment while leaving a larger relationship question: why did he choose her, and what happens next?

Three ways to build a reversal

STATUS

Dismissed → chosen

A public judgment meets a visible act of allegiance.

Example: the empty chair.

MEANING

Threat → care

The same object or gesture acquires a new emotional purpose.

Exercise: a blade first threatens, then cuts a bandage.

PERFORMANCE

Pretend → real

The audience disappears, but the supposedly false gesture continues.

Exercise: a staged touch lingers after witnesses leave.

UNIVERSAL FANTASY · UF

The experience we want.

To be chosen. To be understood. To matter enough.

THE EMPTY CHAIR · OUR SCENE’S UF

“The hottest guy in the room chooses you”

Trope: popular guy / overlooked outsider.

Show it: everyone wants his attention. conversation falters when he enters. Several people seek his attention. Vivienne expects him beside her. He hears her insult, then chooses Linney instead.

First establish that everyone wants his attention.
Then show him choosing the girl who isn’t competing for it.

Her pencil stops.
The girl who mocked her loses her audience.

THE MISSING SETUP · MAKE HIS ATTENTION MATTER

Attractiveness is casting.
Desirability is something the scene demonstrates.

1 · The room notices

He enters. Conversation falters. Several heads turn.

2 · People compete

Someone straightens up. Someone moves a bag off a chair. Vivienne confidently offers hers.

3 · She doesn’t

Linney keeps writing. She isn’t competing for him—and he chooses her anyway.

Universal Fantasy is a framework associated with Theodora Taylor’s 7 Figure Fiction. The distinction here follows her explanation of a trope as the recognizable story situation and UF as its desired emotional appeal. “Universal” does not mean every viewer wants the same thing.
